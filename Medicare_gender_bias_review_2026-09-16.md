# Medicare coverage and payment: sex and gender disparities

Research date: September 16, 2026. Scope: nationwide rules and broad patterns.

**The estradiol–Viagra example does not describe the national basic Medicare drug benefit. There is credible evidence of lower valuation for some procedures involving female anatomy, and substantial variation in access to particular menopause drugs. Neither finding establishes a uniform bias across Medicare.**

This is a nationwide first-pass evidence review, including a new analysis of the public formulary data. It is not a completed fairness audit of every drug, procedure, indication, payment system, or Medicare Advantage contract. The distinction matters: a covered service can still be difficult to obtain or afford, and a difference in price or coverage can have a clinical explanation.

## What was examined

- The August 2026 CMS monthly formulary release: **1,125,804 basic-formulary rows**, covering **328 formularies** linked to **5,499 unsuppressed contract–plan–segment combinations**, representing **5,055 contract–plan pairs**. County repetitions were removed. Twelve selected drug/formulation groups were analyzed across that full published plan universe.
- The 2024 CMS Part D spending table, released June 2026: 14,536 manufacturer/product rows, including 3,625 overall product rows. Selected hormone, menopause-treatment, sildenafil, and Viagra entries were checked.
- The July 2026 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule and five procedure pairs selected from a published comparison.
- Original comparative procedure-payment studies, national coverage policies, and selected local policies relevant to sex or gender.

The monthly formulary files exclude employer, PACE, demonstration plans other than included Medicare-Medicaid Plans, and plans without Part D. Nineteen suppressed plan segments were excluded from the analysis. Results are weighted equally by plan segment, **not by enrollment**, and do not describe the percentage of beneficiaries with access. Source: [CMS monthly formulary data and methodology](https://data.cms.gov/provider-summary-by-type-of-service/medicare-part-d-prescribers/monthly-prescription-drug-plan-formulary-and-pharmacy-network-information).

## 1. Correcting the starting example

**Estradiol is eligible for Part D coverage and widely listed.** In the national screen, every included plan segment listed at least one generic oral estradiol product and generic estradiol vaginal cream. Specific products, strengths, other formulations, patient costs, and claim-level requirements can differ.

Actual utilization also contradicts a blanket exclusion: CMS's 2024 **“Estradiol*” overall product entry** records **4,055,858 claims**, **1,611,030 beneficiaries**, and **$218,158,281.52 in gross drug spending**. This is one CMS grouping, not the total for every estradiol brand/formulation. Gross spending includes plan and patient amounts before rebates; it is not solely federal reimbursement. Source: [CMS 2024 spending source table](https://data.cms.gov/sites/default/files/2026-06/98218f98-166c-4723-8438-c344a4ef96a6/DSD_PTD_RY26_P04_V10_DY24_BGM.csv).

**Viagra used for erectile dysfunction is excluded from the basic Part D benefit.** The statutory exclusion covers drugs used for sexual or erectile dysfunction; another FDA-approved indication can qualify. Sildenafil treatment for pulmonary arterial hypertension therefore cannot be treated as proof of coverage for erectile dysfunction. Supplemental benefits can cover otherwise excluded drugs. Sources: [Social Security Act §1860D-2(e)(2)(A), with supplemental coverage in §1860D-2(a)(2)](https://www.ssa.gov/OP_Home/ssact/title18/1860D-02.htm), [CMS explanation](https://www.cms.gov/Medicare/Prescription-Drug-Coverage/PrescriptionDrugCovContra/Downloads/QAEDDrugs_070606.pdf).

A concrete example of that distinction: the 2026 Pennsylvania PSERS Medicare Plus Rx formulary, published August 5, 2025, places brand Viagra in a bonus-drug section explicitly separate from Medicare-covered Part D drugs. That section also lists Addyi, a female sexual-dysfunction drug. The regular formulary includes estradiol. This is an employer-retiree benefit example, outside the monthly public-file denominator above; its latest September revision was not established. Source: [PSERS formulary, printed pages 64 and 112](https://www.discoverhopbenefits.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/PSERS-Doc-114-CompForm_2026_Medicare-Plus_final_accessible.pdf).

CMS's 2024 spending table also contains **398 Viagra claims** for 92 beneficiaries. Those aggregate records do not identify the indication or explain the benefit basis. Their presence cannot establish routine basic-Part-D coverage for erectile dysfunction; the published record does not resolve why those particular claims appear.

**What this supports:** There is wide coverage of common generic estradiol formulations, alongside much narrower listing of some other menopause options. In this screen, generic estradiol gels were listed considerably less often than generic testosterone gels. That is a useful access question to investigate, not an estimate of discrimination: indications, alternatives, product costs, safety requirements, and clinical need differ.

**What it does not support:** Testosterone is not uniformly unrestricted; its listed options commonly require PA. Low listing of one menopause product does not establish lack of all treatment for its indication. A gel, patch, oral tablet, and vaginal product are not automatically clinically interchangeable. An absence from the formulary does not exclude a successful formulary exception. A missing PA flag does not guarantee an individual claim will be paid.

Affordability needs a separate analysis. For example, the September 2026 Blue Cross NC Essential Plus formulary puts oral estradiol in Tier 1, vaginal cream in Tier 2, patches in Tier 3, and vaginal tablets in Tier 4. Generic status alone does not assure a low tier. Tier numbers are meaningful only with that plan's cost-sharing rules. Source: [actual plan formulary, printed page 58](https://www.myprime.com/content/dam/prime/memberportal/WebDocs/2026/Formularies/MedD/2026%20Blue%20Medicare%20Essential%20Plus%20%28HMO-POS%29%20Formulary.pdf).

## 3. Procedure payments: substantial evidence, but disagreement over matching

**The strongest broad paired-procedure finding:** Penn and colleagues' 2025 study compared 55 pairs using 2023 data. Female procedures had lower work relative value units (RVUs) in 41 pairs. Male procedures averaged 30% higher work RVUs and 25.6% higher physician reimbursement in facility settings. These are selected comparable procedures, not an all-Medicare estimate. “Facility” here describes the setting for the physician payment, not the hospital's separate payment. Source: [original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39978776/).

**Important counterevidence:** Hathaway and colleagues' 2024 study used 10 more strictly analogous pairs, considering service time, postoperative work, global periods, and valuation dates. Seven female procedures had higher work RVUs and three male procedures did; there was no statistically significant difference in work RVUs or work RVUs per minute. A small study finding no significance does not establish equality across every service. Source: [original study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38758183/).

Do not confuse physician compensation with Medicare reimbursement. A 2021 comparison estimated lower hourly compensation for female-specific procedures using **specialty-specific employer compensation per RVU**. Its dollar estimates were not Medicare paying different conversion factors according to the patient's sex. Source: [Polan and Barber, full methods and results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8602770/).

## 5. What a comprehensive bias audit still requires

The highest-priority extensions are procedure valuation, access to clinically appropriate menopause formulations, and gender-marker denials. The national data above identify questions; they do not supply every necessary denominator or causal explanation.

1. **Complete the service inventory.** Include physician payments, inpatient diagnosis-related groups, outpatient hospital and ambulatory surgical-center payments, laboratory services, durable equipment, Part B drugs, and other applicable payment systems. The physician fee schedule alone is not all Medicare. Include covered, excluded, and unlisted services, not just reimbursed claims.
2. **Define clinical comparisons before testing.** Match indication, therapeutic benefit, route, severity, alternatives, evidence quality, procedure time, risk, setting, and postoperative care. Analyze services shared by sexes separately from anatomy-specific pairs. Have independent clinicians review disputed pairs and publish sensitivity analyses using broader and narrower matching rules.
3. **Separate outcomes.** Measure eligibility, formulary inclusion, PA and step therapy, denials, successful exceptions, time to treatment, patient annual cost, physician allowed payment, and hospital payment separately. A low provider payment is not the same outcome as a patient's coverage denial.
4. **Measure actual access and need.** Add rejected and abandoned prescriptions, medical-authorization decisions, appeals, and untreated need. Paid-claim spending cannot show services never received. Adjust for enrollment, age, disability, geography, diagnoses, severity, socioeconomic circumstances, and care setting; report both adjusted and unadjusted gaps.
5. **Analyze Medicare Advantage separately where necessary.** Its provider contracts and utilization management cannot be inferred solely from Original Medicare fee schedules. Distinguish the basic Part D benefit from supplemental and employer-retiree benefits.
6. **Use accurate population variables.** Recorded sex, organ anatomy, and gender identity are different. These public formulary files contain no patient gender identity. Do not assume that use of estradiol identifies a woman or use of testosterone identifies a man.

## Reproducibility and limits

The local `research/` directory retains source tables, selected source metadata, matching definitions, code, and machine-readable findings. The analysis uses exact RxCUI joins; generic-only groups use unbranded clinical-drug concepts and single-ingredient definitions, excluding estrogen/progestin combinations. The broad hormone groups can include ester formulations normalized to the parent hormone in the reference file. Strengths do not create extra plans in the denominator. CMS supplies a proxy NDC for a drug concept, so this is not a manufacturer-by-manufacturer dispensing audit. Source: [CMS 2026 record layout](https://data.cms.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/0564eb37-402d-4110-bd98-2d5399dc30e7/PUFRecordLayout-2026.pdf).

Of 6,140 unique drug identifiers in the basic file, 23 were absent from the contemporaneous reference file, affecting 2,318 of 1,125,804 rows. An independent check of those identifiers using NIH RxNav history records found none belonged to the target ingredients, so this mismatch did not change the target counts. No included plan segment lacked a corresponding formulary. Independent raw-file checks reproduced the plan denominator and selected product counts. No claims about every strength, every age-specific PA rule, every pharmacy, or actual patient cost are made.

The annual spending extraction selects the **Overall** manufacturer row rather than adding it to manufacturer-specific rows. Beneficiary counts are not summed across drugs. Procedure checks retain the setting, payment status, modifier, global period, and RVU components. Source dates differ: 2024 utilization, August 2026 formularies, July 2026 payment schedules, and policy versions effective by September 16, 2026.

**Defensible conclusion:** Selected studies support concern about undervaluation of some care involving female anatomy. Current formularies show meaningful gaps between menopause products, while common generic estradiol remains widely listed and testosterone also faces restrictions. A nationwide finding of discriminatory reimbursement across all Medicare services is not established by the available analysis.
