# Bone screening: provisional AI extraction, September 21, 2026

Two records were assessed: the assigned study and exactly one original study from its references. This is provisional AI screening/extraction, not independent human review or a completed systematic review. Neither cohort establishes cisgender identity. **Gyftopoulos et al., 2024 — PMID 38459983; proposed root ID L-R03.** Metadata is verified, but access was limited to the abstract and publisher preview. Among 80,439 Asian American Medicare beneficiaries, 12,102 received DXA (15.1%). Women had 19.8% screening versus 5.0% for men (P<.001); sex-specific counts were unavailable. Eligibility began at **65 for women and 70 for men**. This crude contrast concerns the Asian American subgroup, not all Medicare beneficiaries. The reported adjusted result compares Asian American with non-Asian American beneficiaries, not women with men. Study years, enrollment restrictions, need adjustment and follow-up require full text. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38459983/) · [Publisher](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00256-024-04643-1).

**Gillespie and Morin, 2017 — PMID 27884649; proposed root ID L-R04.** Reference 9 of the first paper. The publisher-formatted, openly licensed main article was readable on ResearchGate; supplements and a downloaded PDF were not reviewed. MEDLINE and Crossref corroborate its canonical title, authors and DOI. The study includes **1,638,454 women**, of whom **588,737 had Medicare Advantage**. Tables 1–2 provide separable MA results for bone mass measurement during the latest two years of enrollment:

| Medicare Advantage subgroup | Denominator | Screening, % (95% CI) |
|---|---:|---:|
| All women ≥50 | 588,737 | 22.9 (22.8–23.0) |
| Ages 50–64 | 58,285 | 19.6 (19.3–19.9) |
| Ages 65–79 | 394,791 | 26.8 (26.6–26.9) |
| Ages ≥80 | 135,661 | 12.9 (12.7–13.1) |

These are utilization estimates in women, not sex comparisons. Numerators were not reconstructed from rounded rates. The measure includes bone measurement methods beyond DXA. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27884649/) · [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amjmed.2016.10.018) · [Open article text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/310667253_Trends_and_disparities_in_osteoporosis_screening_among_women_in_the_United_States_2008-2014_Declines_in_utilization_among_women_50_to_64_years_old_and_persistent_underutilization_among_women_65_and_ol).

Useful counterevidence: adjusted Asian-versus-white screening odds were higher at ages 65–79 (aOR 1.18; 95% CI 1.13–1.24), but null at ≥80 (0.97; 0.86–1.11). These adjusted models combine MA and commercial patients. Differences from the first paper involve different populations and comparisons. In ages 65–79, the unadjusted rates changed little (26.2% to 26.8%), while the adjusted 2013-versus-2008 odds ratio was 0.86 (0.84–0.89). Years identify the start of two-year observation windows.

Neither paper directly measures denied claims, payment amounts or a causal reimbursement-policy effect. Prior test results, clinical risk and life expectancy can affect appropriate testing; two-year nonreceipt does not automatically establish unmet need. The second paper's discussion of universal two-year screening must not be imported as current clinical guidance. Gender identity remains unreported, and MA findings cannot be generalized to fee-for-service Medicare.
